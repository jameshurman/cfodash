Part one

The CFO's
marketing problem

The role of the CFO has changed more in the last decade than in the four before it. The job is no longer to report on the past and steward the present. It is to allocate capital toward the future. Most CFOs are now directly involved in shaping decisions on R&D, M&A, digital transformation, and physical CapEx. Full partners, not auditors.

There is one exception.

Survey finding · Q1 · n=528

When asked across eight major investment categories how directly they shape decisions, APAC CFOs report being a primary decision-maker or sharing responsibility in 74% of M&A decisions, 80% of risk management, and 90% of FP&A. For marketing and brand building, only 38% report high involvement - the lowest of any category except people and talent.

This is not a capability gap. CFOs are no less equipped to evaluate marketing than they are to evaluate a manufacturing line upgrade or an ERP system. It is a framing gap. Marketing has historically been presented to finance as an operating expense to be managed at the lowest efficient cost, with payback expected inside the financial year. That framing has been internalised on both sides. Marketing teams pre-emptively justify spend against immediate returns. Finance teams measure spend against immediate returns. The conversation never reaches the longer time horizons where most of the value is actually created.

The CFOs who've closed this gap did not do it by becoming marketers. They did it by treating marketing the way they treat any other meaningful investment - asking what return it generates, over what period, against what hurdle, and at what level of competitive intensity.

The challenges CFOs name

When we asked 528 APAC senior finance leaders to describe their single biggest challenge working with the marketing function, the responses clustered around a consistent theme. Not that marketing doesn't matter. That its returns come expressed in a language finance cannot readily evaluate.

Open-ended responses · Q10 · verbatim

"Finance is all about accurate measurements, whereas marketing does not have strong metrics."  |  "The most difficult task is reconciling the requirements of evidence-based decision making with the truth that some of the most successful marketing results such as perception and trust cannot be quantified."  |  "While the finance department demands certainty in terms of return on investment, the results of marketing may be somewhat unpredictable."

The concerns CFOs are prioritising as growth barriers compound this. Economic volatility, AI disruption, shareholder short-term pressure. Those are the top three. What's absent from the list is competitive market share loss - which is the actual mechanism by which most of these organisations will lose ground over the next two years, and against which consistent marketing investment is one of the few durable defences.

Part two

Perception vs
reality

The gap between what CFOs believe about marketing and what the evidence shows is not a matter of degree. It is a systematic mismatch across four specific areas. Each one produces a predictable underinvestment decision.

Perception 1: marketing returns are mostly in-period

Most APAC CFOs expect marketing to break even within 12 months. That expectation is a direct product of how marketing has always been measured and reported. And it's wrong.

63%
of APAC CFOs prioritise advertising spend that can generate ROI within the same financial year - a position the econometric evidence shows is calibrated to observe less than half of marketing's actual return.

The data is clear on this. Between 50% and 70% of advertising's total profit return is delivered after the campaign window closes. Google's Effectiveness Equation - a meta-analysis across 167 studies in the UK, Germany, France, and Italy - found that only 30-50% of return arrives in the first four months. The remaining 50-70% arrives across the following twenty months.

Source: Google, The Effectiveness Equation (2025), Figure 10

The implication is direct. A company measuring marketing on a 12-month window is observing somewhere between a quarter and a half of the total return - and treating it as the whole. Every budget decision made on that basis is being made with most of the data missing.

Perception 2: marketing should pay back faster than any other investment

There is a striking inconsistency in how CFOs apply payback expectations. When asked over what period each major investment type reaches break-even, they apply longer, more patient horizons to every category. Except one.

34% of CFOs expect marketing to break even within 12 months - the highest of any investment category. Only 37% are willing to wait 25 months or more, compared to 52–59% for CapEx, Digital/IT, and R&D.

This produces an internal contradiction that the data makes visible. 69% of the same CFOs agree that "marketing should be seen as an investment in the future of the business rather than a short-term expense." Yet 34% simultaneously expect it to pay back within a single year. You cannot coherently hold both positions at once.

The measurement consequence

When you measure a return profile against a horizon shorter than its actual payback period, the returns outside the window are invisible. A company evaluating marketing on a 12-month window will rationally underinvest relative to its true contribution - not because the returns aren't there, but because its measurement framework cannot see them. The problem is not in the investment. It is in the ruler.

The actual payback profile, established across large-scale econometric studies on advertising returns, is 13–24 months. Profit Ability 2 found that the average advertising spend returns £4.11 in profit over 24 months. By comparison, typical CapEx investments deliver 0.3–0.6× compounded over the same period.

Source: Thinkbox, Profit Ability 2 (2024), Figure 60
Source: Thinkbox, Profit Ability 2 (2024), Figure 12

Marketing's full 24-month ROI exceeds comparable capital investment returns by approximately 49% on an annualised basis when measured correctly. The accounting treatment (marketing must be expensed in-period rather than capitalised) does not change this economic reality. It only obscures it.

Perception 3: performance marketing is the safe bet

In most APAC organisations, performance marketing has become the default allocation. It is measurable, attributable, and delivers numbers within the financial year. From a capital allocation standpoint, it appears to meet the CFO's requirements perfectly.

But that apparent safety is an artefact of measurement, not economics. In any category at any given time, only 11–26% of potential buyers are actively in-market. Performance marketing captures this group well. What it cannot do is reach the 74–89% of future buyers who will enter the market later. Their eventual choice is being shaped right now - by brand investment, not by search bids.

Source: Hurman, Future Demand (2026), survey of n=500 nationally representative US consumers

The relationship is quantified by a 2024 study from Tracksuit and TikTok: brands with high awareness (80% of category buyers) convert the same performance advertising at 2.96 times the rate of brands with low awareness (20%). The same pound of search spend generates almost three times the return when supported by sufficient brand investment. Performance marketing harvests demand. Brand marketing creates the conditions for demand to be harvestable. A company that funds only the harvest is mining its existing brand equity without replenishing it. That works for a while. A company that funds only the harvest is mining its existing brand equity without replenishing it. That works for a while.

Source: Hurman/Tracksuit/TikTok, The Awareness Advantage (2024)

Google's Effectiveness Equation analysed European e-commerce ROI across thousands of campaigns and found that peak ROI sits where brand spending makes up 47–63% of total media. Most APAC organisations are allocating well outside this band.

Source: Ipsos MMA Meta Analysis, Marketing Mix Database, 2020–2023 · Google, The Effectiveness Equation (2025), Figure 12

Perception 4: cutting marketing is a clean saving

When conditions tighten, marketing is typically one of the first lines reviewed for reduction. The logic feels sound: the savings are immediate, the spend is discretionary, and the consequences are invisible inside the financial year.

$1.92
required in future spend to recover every $1 saved by cutting brand marketing today - up from $1.85 in 2023, a 4% increase in two years as brand-building costs rise in fragmented media environments. (BCG, 2025)

The Ehrenberg-Bass Institute analysed 57 cases of brands that stopped or substantially reduced advertising. The average outcome: a 16% sales decline after one year, a 25% decline after two. When those brands resumed advertising, they did so at a higher cost-per-acquisition - because mental availability had decayed and had to be rebuilt from scratch.

Marketing investment is also a relative game. The principle of Excess Share of Voice - established in research by Jon Jones in 1990 and replicated consistently since - holds that when a brand's share of advertising exceeds its market share, it tends to grow. When it is lower, it tends to lose. The cut is not assessed in isolation. It is assessed relative to what competitors are doing.

Source: Binet/Field, The Long and the Short of It (2013)
Part three

What the best-in-class
CFOs do differently

The survey data does not just document the perception-reality gap. It identifies, with statistical precision, what separates the CFOs at outperforming organisations from those falling behind. The result is a clear and replicable pattern.

Across all 528 respondents, we built a composite score from the five variables that most strongly correlate with competitive performance. We call it the capital asset mental model score. It predicts whether a CFO's organisation outperforms or underperforms its competitors with a correlation of r=0.322 (p<0.001). For a survey-based measure of a mental model, that is a substantial predictive signal.

The five dimensions that separate them

The composite score is built from five measurable dimensions, each drawn from a different part of the survey. Together they describe not just different attitudes but different operational realities.

Dimension 1: What they expect marketing to return. The single strongest predictor in the dataset. Pipeline-model CFOs (Q1) expect marketing to return a mean of 1.95× in the short term - barely above break-even. Asset-model CFOs (Q5) expect 5.68×. The long-term gap is wider still: 2.50× vs 6.73×. No other investment type shows this pattern - CapEx, Digital/IT, and R&D expectations are nearly flat across all quintiles. The difference is entirely specific to marketing.

The paradox that explains the gap

The most important finding in the dataset is also the most counterintuitive. Below-performing CFOs are more likely to agree that "marketing should be seen as an investment in the future of the business rather than a short-term expense" - 4.18 vs 3.94 for above-performers.

They say the right thing. Their behaviour tells a different story. Their ROI expectations are lower. Their investment portfolios are more concentrated in performance marketing. Their perceived returns are transactional. Their breakeven horizons are shorter.

The below-performing CFO has absorbed the language of long-term marketing investment without the financial mental model that gives it meaning. The above-performing CFO lives it without needing to articulate it. The gap is not knowledge. It is cognition. And it is measurable.

Part four

How to close
the gap

Five principles distinguish the CFOs who get marketing capital allocation right from those who do not. None requires the finance team to acquire marketing expertise. All require it to apply the same standards to marketing that it already applies to every other major investment.

Survey finding · Q6 · n=528

A majority of APAC CFOs rate their marketing function below expectations on long-term investment strategy, specifically on balancing short-term performance marketing against long-term brand building. 33% rate it below expectations on this dimension. The same majority agree that CFOs and CMOs "often talk a different language" on marketing investment and measurement. The diagnostic is correct. What is missing is the evidence base on which to act.

01

Measure across the full payback window

Marketing's true return is delivered over approximately 24 months. Internal evaluation should reflect that horizon - even though external reporting treats it as an in-period expense. These two things are not in conflict. The company should know what marketing actually delivers, even if the accounting framework requires it to be recognised differently.

In practice, this means commissioning Marketing Mix Modelling (MMM), which captures both short and long-term effects, rather than relying solely on attribution-based tools that only see in-period response. The asset-model CFOs in our data have longer, more deliberate ROI horizons specifically for marketing - and nowhere else.

See Figure 9 - Dimension 5

The breakeven horizon data shows this directly: asset-model CFOs cluster in the 13–24 month window that matches actual advertising payback profiles. Pipeline-model CFOs expect payback within 12 months. Before most of the return has arrived.

02

Allocate by demand structure, not attribution-ease

In every category, current and future demand exist in known proportions: roughly 11–26% of potential buyers are in-market right now, and 74–89% are not. The performance marketing budget should be sized to capture the available current demand. Once that is done, additional performance spend produces diminishing returns. The remaining budget should fund brand investment that biases future buyers toward the brand before they enter the market.

The optimal split, identified consistently across decades of global effectiveness research and confirmed in the most recent APAC-specific data, is around 50% brand and 50% performance. The survey data shows that below-performing CFOs are heavily over-indexed on performance marketing. The shift required is not to abandon performance. It is to stop letting it crowd out everything else.

03

Invest competitively

Marketing investment is a relative game. The relevant benchmark is not last year's budget, not a fixed percentage of revenue. It is the company's share of voice relative to its share of market, set against direct competitors. When share of voice exceeds market share, market share tends to grow. When share of voice is lower, market share tends to decline - even if absolute spend is unchanged.

The CFO survey data carries an important nuance here. Across the five mental model quintiles, marketing spend as a percentage of revenue rises consistently from the most pipeline-oriented organisations to the most asset-oriented: from 10.6% to 13.6%. Asset-model CFOs don't just think about marketing differently. They back it with more capital. These organisations are not only making more productive use of their marketing spend - they are starting with more of it.

04

Agree objectives and measurement first

64% of APAC CFOs agree that "CMOs and CFOs often talk a different language when it comes to collaborating on marketing investment and measurement." 65% say marketing should be able to show how advertising translates into profit more clearly. Only 57% say their organisation has a reliable approach to evaluating advertising ROI.

The challenge is usually not that marketing is failing, or that finance is unreasonable. It is that both functions are succeeding against different definitions of success. Marketing optimises for revenue or new customer acquisition. Finance focuses on profitable revenue, margin growth, or customer lifetime value. Without a shared objective defined upfront, every measurement conversation becomes a dispute about whose numbers are right.

The three measurement tools that provide the foundation for a shared framework are described below. None requires marketing expertise from the finance team. Each is a standard analytical discipline applied to a new domain.

Tool 1
Marketing Mix Modelling

Isolates how much incremental revenue each channel actually drove, stripped of seasonality and external noise. Captures both short and long-term effects - unlike attribution, which sees only in-period response.

Tool 2
Incrementality testing

Geo-lift or conversion-lift experiments that prove causal impact. Particularly important for brand investment, where attribution tools systematically undercount long-term effects.

Tool 3
LTV integration

Passing customer lifetime value signals back into media optimisation, reorienting the entire marketing system toward the metric finance actually cares about - not clicks or impressions, but long-run customer value.

Building that shared foundation also requires a structured joint operating rhythm. Three cadences:

Annual · Strategic portfolio setup

Agree the marketing hurdle rate (WACC plus a risk premium), lock in the financial model linking marketing to CLV and margin, and categorise the budget into three investment buckets: core/defensive (immediate cash flow), growth/expansion (12–36 month NPV), and experimental (venture-capital logic, optional write-off).

Quarterly · Portfolio rebalancing

Review MMM data to compare predicted vs actual cash flow by channel. Check whether longer-horizon brand campaigns are tracking to their milestone markers. Release the next tranche of capital based on performance - rather than releasing the full annual budget upfront.

Monthly · Operational efficiency

Review lead metrics (CPA trends, retention rates) as early warning signals for future cash flows. Allow fast-cycle reallocation within pre-approved quarterly buckets. Update the rolling 3-month forecast of marketing-driven cash inflows so the CFO can accurately adjust working capital requirements.

05

Build in demand flexibility

The four principles above set the strategic and measurement foundations. There is also a tactical dimension that has grown more material since 2020 - the speed at which demand moves. In most categories, demand now fluctuates substantially within a year, sometimes within a quarter. Annual budgets set in January are frequently misaligned with actual demand by June.

Google's research with Analytic Partners across 80 retail brands in Australia and New Zealand quantified the cost of this directly. For every $100 of revenue earned from paid search, a further $39 on average was missed due to lost impression share. Of that $39, fully $20 was attributable to budget constraints alone - money not available when demand was present.

Survey finding · Q3 · n=528

A majority of APAC CFOs identify outdated budgeting processes that cannot adapt to real-time market demand as a material barrier to growth. The capability to respond is being built. The budgeting model has not yet caught up.

The asymmetry
that should change
the default

The cost of overinvesting in marketing is small. It is recognised in the same financial period as the spend. It can be detected through standard MMM analysis within months and corrected in the next budget cycle. It is a self-correcting error.

The cost of underinvesting is the opposite. It is large. It is delayed. It compounds. By the time it shows up in declining sales, weaker pricing power, and lost market share, the damage has typically been accruing for one to two years. The recovery cost, BCG estimates, is $1.92 for every $1 saved - and that figure has been rising.

Our survey of 528 APAC CFOs shows that the outperforming organisations are not those spending the most on marketing. They are those whose senior finance leaders hold a different theory of what marketing is - an asset that builds durable value, not a tap you turn on when you need transactions.

The gap between what marketing actually returns and what most CFOs believe it returns is wide, durable, and costly. The CFOs who close it first will compound an advantage their peers will find difficult to close.

The evidence is available. The framework is clear. The case for action, on the data, is hard to argue with.

What remains is the decision.
